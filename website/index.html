Constrained Whole-Body Tracking for Humanoid Robots

Daniel Morton1· Pranit Mohnot1· Marco Pavone1,2
1Stanford University 2NVIDIA Research
Paper arXiv Code

Where does safety fit into a learning-based humanoid motion tracking stack? We approach safety from both the kinematics and dynamics levels, addressing safety from both sides (input and output) of the policy. On the kinematics side, constraints can naturally fit into the IK-based retargeting process between human and robot form-factors, or be applied as a safety filter on motions already mapped to the robot geometry. On the dynamics side, a potentially unsafe output from the policy can be passed through a safety filter prior to execution on the robot.

Abstract

Recent advances in reinforcement learning (RL) have demonstrated impressive whole-body agility for humanoid robots, yet ensuring safety and satisfying constraints – particularly those specified after training – remains a challenge. Towards this goal, we present ConstrainedMimic, a control framework that leverages whole-body kinematics and dynamics for real-time constraint enforcement within RL tracking policies. By integrating principles from operational space control and control barrier functions (CBFs), we enable the satisfaction of arbitrary runtime constraints on both the kinematic reference motion and the underlying dynamics. In whole-body motion-tracking and teleoperation experiments on a Unitree G1 with a learned policy, we demonstrate collision avoidance (both with the robot body and external obstacles), joint limits, and center of mass stability constraints. By remaining consistent with the current contact mode and tracking objectives, we minimally restrict the capabilities of the policy when constraints are active. Our method is fully differentiable, runs on CPU, GPU, and TPU, and can be deployed at kilohertz control rates.

Overview

Purely kinematic notions of safety are insufficient for policies with tracking error

Given an unsafe input to a whole-body tracking policy (a kinematic reference), safety constraints can be applied at this level either via a kinematic filter, or via constrained retargeting. While this filtered reference may satisfy constraints, deploying it on the robot does not necessarily lead to safe behavior, due to not accounting for inertial effects, overshoot, and general tracking errors of the underlying policy.

Here, we demonstrate a "karate chop" motion where we enforce a constraint between the hand and a planar surface. Although kinematic safety methods can constrain an unsafe behavior (A) and produce a safe reference (B), tracking this with a general motion tracker (C) leads to overshoot and constraint violation. Accounting for dynamics (D/E) maintains safety, whether the original input was safe or not.

A safety filter is beneficial even for conditions known at training time

While a key benefit of an online safety filter is the ability to enforce runtime-specified constraints that were unknown at training time, these filters are still useful for conditions that are known at training time.

Consider self-collision avoidance: the full model of the robot kinematics and link geometry is known during training, and often, self-collisions are heavily penalized in the reward function. Still, the tracking policy provides no guarantee that this constraint is implicitly represented, and tracking an unsafe reference (A) can still lead to collisions (B) without a filter (C).

Safety filters for humanoid whole-body control must account for the contact-constrained kinematics and dynamics

For safety filters to work properly, they must have an accurate model of the kinematics (for first-order, input-level filters) and dynamics (for second-order, output-level filters). Naively enforcing safety without considering the contact state, though, will result in a mismatch that leads to unsafe and unexpected behavior.

Consider an antigravity lean, which violates a center of mass stability constraint. Unless we account for both the contact-constrained kinematics and a task-consistent objective for this motion (E), we either get physically-implausible, unsafe, or undesired motions from the filtered output (A-D).

Discrete kinematic modes impact the dynamic feasibility of single-step constraint enforcement

CBFs are a single-step method of enforcing safety, and some constraints inherently require longer-horizon reasoning over discrete actions or modes.

Consider walking under an obstacle at head-height: if whole-body collision avoidance is enforced during the motion, the resulting output is not necessarily dynamically-feasible, depending on the kinematic mode of the robot when the constraint is active. A head-on approach leads to an unstable "limbo" motion, whereas approaching it from a slight crouch can allow the robot to further duck under the obstacle, maintaining a stable posture throughout.

Certain constraints inherently require breaking the current contact mode to maintain safety

Consider a humanoid standing at rest with a dynamic obstacle approaching. With the feet fixed in a double-support contact mode, the robot cannot locomote, inevitably leading to a collision! In cases like this, breaking the current contact mode is necessary to maintain safety.

To address this, we propose a simple bilevel heuristic: a reduced-order planar dynamics model runs in parallel to a whole-body safety method, with a lower-body planner (e.g. from SONIC) generating the full kinematic reference to track both the desired upper-body motion and the safe planar motion.

On hardware, enforcing safety at both the kinematics and dynamics levels most reliably delivers a safe output, even under minor model mismatch.

On the Unitree G1 hardware, we recreate our "karate chop" experiment, seeking to understand how the results differ during real hardware deployment. As in our simulation experiments, the kinematic filter alone leads to small constraint violation, but under minor dynamics mismatch, the dynamic filter alone also leads to minor violation (whereas in simulation, this was the least conservative approach that maintained safety).

In general, we find that kinematic and dynamic safety methods complement eachother for reliable constraint enforcement: kinematics alone fails due to a lack of consideration of inertia and tracking error; dynamics alone fails due to imperfect modeling and estimation at runtime; considering both effects together ultimately leads to safe behavior from the robot.

All methods run well above policy frequency, even on edge devices and with hundreds of constraints

Even with upwards of 300 constraints, our filters can be deployed at well over 1 kHz on desktop computers, and over 100 Hz on edge devices such as an Orin Nano.
